Most teaching days are planned from the agenda outwards: a date, a room, some speakers, a feedback form. Afterwards there are photos, packs and a pile of smile sheets, and nobody can say what changed.

Plan it the other way round. **Start with the change, not the event.** Answer these six questions, in this order, before anyone books a room.

**1. Who are the learners, and where will they use this?**\
What can they already do? What gets in their way at work: time, rotas, kit, access needs?\
*Watch for:* "everyone in the service". If you can't picture the learner, you can't design for them.

**2. What's the need?**\
What happens now, and what should happen instead? Is that gap something teaching can fix, or is it really about staffing, equipment or policy?\
*Watch for:* a training day as the answer to a system problem.

**3. What should people be able to do afterwards?**\
Write one to three outcomes with verbs you could observe: *explain*, *demonstrate*, *decide*, *escalate*.\
*Watch for:* "be aware of", "understand", "appreciate". You can't see any of those, so you'll never know if they happened.

**4. How will you know?**\
What would show it worked, on the day and a month later? Who will notice?\
*Watch for:* attendance lists, smile sheets and photos. They show the day *happened*, not that anything *changed*.

**5. What will learners do?**\
What will they practise, discuss or make, so they leave able to do the thing in question 3?\
*Watch for:* an agenda that's a list of speakers. Watching isn't practising.

**6. What happens after the day?**\
What will people revisit at 30, 60 and 90 days? Who will hold them to it, and what support will that person give (time, release, a slot at the team meeting)? Who owns the follow-up once the day is over?\
*Watch for:* commitments nobody follows up, and a Teams channel nobody opens.

---

**If you can only do one thing:** answer question 3 before you write the agenda. Everything else gets easier once you know what people should be able to *do*.

## The bigger picture

**Training is a system, not an event.** The research on workplace training says the same thing again and again: what happens before and after the day matters, not just the day itself (Salas et al. 2012). Education standards in the NHS and in universities say it too, in their own words.

A widely used planning model in medical education is Kern's six steps (Thomas, Kern et al. 2022). The six questions above are a plain-English version of it:

| Kern's six steps | The six questions |
|---|---|
| 1. Problem identification and general needs assessment | 2. What's the need? |
| 2. Targeted needs assessment | 1. Who are the learners? |
| 3. Goals and objectives | 3. What should they be able to do? |
| 4. Educational strategies | 5. What will learners do? |
| 5. Implementation | 6. What happens after the day? |
| 6. Evaluation and feedback | 4. How will you know? |

Kern's steps work as a cycle: what you learn from step 6 feeds the next version.

**Where Teaching That Lands fits.** The [three sessions](/sessions) teach people who teach to answer questions 3 to 6 well, for one session of their own. They don't cover an organisation's needs analysis, co-production, formal assessment, preparing educators, or keeping teaching under review. Those belong to an education lead.

**What an education lead brings.** The judgement behind those rows. Knowing when a request isn't a teaching problem. Saying "not like that", with reasons. If you don't have one, the [Teaching Design Check](/facilitator/design-check) shows what's going unanswered.

**Going further.** For the people who teach: a postgraduate certificate in health or clinical education; NHS England's Nursing and Midwifery Educator Framework; or Advance HE Fellowship, professional recognition designed around university teaching, and useful for anyone in a split clinical and university role. [Portfolio Mapping](/facilitator/portfolio-mapping) shows how the programme can count towards the last two.
