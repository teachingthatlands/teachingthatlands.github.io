Most teaching days are planned from the agenda outwards: a date, a room, some speakers, a feedback form. Afterwards there are photos, packs and a pile of smile sheets, and nobody can say what changed.

Plan it the other way round. **Start with the change, not the event.** Answer these six questions, in this order, before anyone books a room.

**1. Who are the learners, and where will they use this?**\
What can they already do? What gets in their way at work: time, rotas, kit, access needs?\
*Watch for:* "everyone in the service". If you can't picture the learner, you can't design for them.

**2. What's the need?**\
What happens now, and what should happen instead? Is that gap something teaching can fix, or is it really about staffing, equipment or policy?\
*Watch for:* a training day as the answer to a system problem.

**3. What should people be able to do afterwards?**\
Write one to three outcomes with verbs you could observe: *explain*, *demonstrate*, *decide*, *escalate*.\
*Watch for:* "be aware of", "understand", "appreciate". You can't see any of those, so you'll never know if they happened.

**4. How will you know?**\
What would show it worked, on the day and a month later? Who will notice?\
*Watch for:* attendance lists, smile sheets and photos. They show the day *happened*, not that anything *changed*.

**5. What will learners do?**\
What will they practise, discuss or make, so they leave able to do the thing in question 3?\
*Watch for:* an agenda that's a list of speakers. Watching isn't practising.

**6. What happens after the day?**\
What will people revisit at 30, 60 and 90 days? Who will hold them to it, and what support will that person give (time, release, a slot at the team meeting)? Who owns the follow-up once the day is over?\
*Watch for:* commitments nobody follows up, and a Teams channel nobody opens.

---

**If you can only do one thing:** answer question 3 before you write the agenda. Everything else gets easier once you know what people should be able to *do*.
