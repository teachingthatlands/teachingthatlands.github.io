Most teaching days are planned from the agenda outwards: a date, a room, some speakers, a feedback form. Afterwards there are photos, packs and a pile of smile sheets, and nobody can say what changed.

Plan it the other way round. **Start with the change, not the event.** Ask these three questions before anyone books a room.

**1. What should people do differently, and why don't they do it now?**\
What would show it had changed, ideally something you already measure? Could they do it if their life depended on it? If so, it isn't a skills problem: look at the kit, staffing, workload, the form or the culture.\
*Watch for:* the request arriving as a format ("a study day on X"), and a date set before anyone has named the problem. → [Is training the answer?](/theories/is-training-the-answer)

**2. What will they be able to do afterwards, and how will you know?**\
One to three things, with verbs you could watch: *explain*, *demonstrate*, *decide*, *escalate*. Then: what would show they can, on the day and a month later?\
*Watch for:* "be aware of", "understand", "appreciate". You can't see any of those, so you'll never know if they happened. And attendance lists and smile sheets show the day *happened*, not that anything *changed*. → [Miller's pyramid](/theories/knowing-isnt-doing)

**3. Who will ask them about it in a month, and what support will they give?**\
Do managers know what's being taught, and what they'll be asked to support? Will people get a chance to use it within days? Who will ask what changed, and give the time, release or slot at the team meeting it needs?\
*Watch for:* "we'll email the slides", and managers hearing about it afterwards, if at all. → [What happens back at work](/theories/back-at-work)

**If you can only do one thing:** ask question 1. Some requests turn out not to be teaching problems at all, and that saves the most time.

Hand it to whoever will plan the teaching.
