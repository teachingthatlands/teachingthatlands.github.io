Standard practice in most educational settings is *blocking*: do all the problems of type A, then all the problems of type B. It feels efficient. Progress within each block is visible and fast. Students feel they're getting it.

But on a later test, mixed practice usually does better. When problems are mixed, students can't rely on the surrounding context to identify what strategy to use — they have to *recognise* what kind of problem they're facing. That extra step, which blocking removes, is actually a critical component of the skill being developed.

Interleaved practice — mixing problems, topics, or cases — forces learners to discriminate between types and to select the appropriate approach. It's harder, slower, and produces less apparent progress session-by-session. On later tests it usually beats blocked practice, especially when learners must tell similar-looking types apart. The benefit is moderate on average and does not show up for every kind of material. Real clinical situations don't come labelled, so there are good reasons to expect it to help, though this hasn't been tested much in clinical settings.

## What this means for teaching

- **Mix case types rather than grouping them** — a series of "here are three respiratory cases" is blocked. Interspersing respiratory, cardiac, and metabolic cases across the same session is interleaved.
- **Expect the resistance** — learners will feel that interleaved practice is harder and less productive. That feeling of difficulty is normal and doesn't mean it isn't working. Name it explicitly so it doesn't become discouragement.
- **Interleave within and across sessions** — within a session, mix the kinds of case or problem so learners have to work out which is which, rather than doing all of one kind first. Across sessions, return to earlier material in new forms.
- **The mismatch between performance and learning** — blocked practice looks better *during* practice; mixed practice usually does better on the test afterwards. Teach your learners to mistrust their feeling of fluency during practice.
