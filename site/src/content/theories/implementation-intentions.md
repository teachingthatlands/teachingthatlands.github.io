Most professional development ends with good intentions. Participants leave energised, intend to change something, and then don't — not because they lack motivation, but because intention without implementation structure evaporates on contact with the actual working day.

Peter Gollwitzer's research showed something simple and powerful: if you link an intention to a specific situation — *when I see X, I will do Y* — people are noticeably more likely to follow through. Pooling 94 studies, Gollwitzer and Sheeran (2006) found a medium-to-large effect (d = 0.65); later and larger reviews find a smaller effect, but still a useful one. The situational cue acts as an automatic trigger, bypassing the need to remember and decide in the moment.

This is not a motivational technique. It's a cognitive one. Implementation intentions work by creating a mental association between a context and a response. When the situation comes up, the planned response is much easier to start, often with little conscious effort. Which is precisely what you need when you're mid-ward-round, mid-consultation, or mid-whatever-clinical-reality-looks-like-that-day.

## What this means for teaching

- **End every session with a plan, not a reflection** — "What will you do differently?" is weaker than "When will you next teach something? What is one thing you'll change? What will prompt you to do it?" The specificity is the mechanism.
- **If-then prompts are a closing activity** — "If I'm preparing a teaching session and I notice I have more than five slides, then I will..." takes three minutes to write and significantly increases the probability of the behaviour.
- **Intentions decay; plans persist** — the days straight after a session are when good intentions most easily slip, before new habits have had a chance to form. A written plan helps protect them.
- **The clinical environment is full of cues** — bedside, ward round, handover, debrief. Helping participants identify *when* and *where* they'll apply new approaches is more useful than exhortation to apply them generally.
