Ebbinghaus mapped the forgetting curve in 1885 and the finding has held ever since: memory decays steeply soon after learning, then levels off; each revisit resets the curve and makes it shallower. The antidote is not more intensive initial study — it's *distribution*. Learning the same material across multiple sessions, with gaps between them, gives reliably better long-term retention than cramming the same total study time into one go.

Nobody is completely sure why spacing works. A leading explanation is that coming back after a gap makes you work to retrieve the material, and that effort strengthens the memory. Animal studies point to processes in the brain that need time between sessions, but this hasn't been shown directly for classroom learning. Space it correctly and you can achieve durable retention with surprisingly little total study time.

The problem for clinical education is structural: most teaching is episodic. A session happens, attendees leave, and the content is never revisited in a deliberate way. That's massed practice by default.

## What this means for teaching

- **Revisit deliberately** — build retrieval of previous session content into the opening of every new one. Not a summary (passive) but a recall task (active).
- **Teach less, return more** — it is usually better to cover fewer topics and come back to them than to cover everything once and never return. Ruthless curation enables spaced return.
- **Exploit the forgetting curve** — revisit after a gap, when recall takes some effort but is still possible (a message, a follow-up question, a reflection prompt). As a rough guide, a day or two later suits remembering something for a week. For something that needs to last months, add later reminders weeks apart.
- **Design for return** — if the programme has multiple sessions, use early ones as content to retrieve in later ones. The sessions themselves are spaced practice for their own material. This programme's sessions are a week apart, and Session 2 opens with a Kahoot on Session 1.
