**The idea in one sentence:** the same amount of learning, spread out with gaps in between, lasts longer than cramming it all into one go. We forget fast at first and then more slowly, and each time we come back to something, it seems to fade more slowly than before.

## You've seen this already

Think about learning to swim, or to play the guitar. One four-hour lesson would wear you out, and you'd forget most of it by the next month. Four one-hour lessons, a week apart, would get you much further. The total time is the same. The gaps make the difference.

Or think about revising for an exam. Cramming the night before can get you through the next morning. A month later, most of it has gone. The people who revised a bit each week usually still know it.

## Why it matters for your teaching

A lot of healthcare teaching happens once. There's an induction day, a study day, or a yearly mandatory update. People leave, and nobody comes back to it on purpose. That's cramming by default.

Say a new policy changes how your team records falls. You could run one long briefing and hope it sticks. Or you could brief people once, then ask one question about it at the huddle a few days later, and another a few weeks after that. The second plan needn't take more time, and it is likely to keep more of it alive.

Start with what people need to do differently, and for how long. If a porter or a receptionist needs to remember a new process for a week, a reminder a day or two later is about right. If a nurse needs to keep a skill all year, plan reminders a few weeks apart. The longer it has to last, the longer the gaps can be.

## Try this

- **Open with last time.** Start each session by asking people to recall the last one. Make it a quick question they answer, not a summary slide they read.
- **Teach less, return more.** It is usually better to cover fewer topics and come back to them than to cover everything once and never return.
- **Plan the follow-up before the session.** Decide now what you'll ask people later, and when: a message, a question at the next team meeting, a quick check on shift.
- **Leave a real gap.** Come back when remembering takes some effort but is still possible. A quick check at the end of a session is useful too, but it doesn't replace one after a gap.

## How sure are we?

This is one of the oldest and best-repeated findings about memory. A large review pulled together 317 experiments and found a clear overall benefit (Cepeda et al., 2006). On average, people recalled about 47% after spaced study, against about 37% after studying in one go, for the same total time. So the gain is solid and reliable, but not magic.

Most of those studies used word lists or facts, in labs, with students. There is much less research on hands-on clinical skills or on staff learning at work. The advice about how long to leave the gaps comes from a few lab studies, so treat it as a rough guide, not a rule.

Nobody is completely sure why spacing works. A leading explanation is that coming back after a gap makes you work to remember the material, and that effort strengthens the memory. Animal studies point to processes in the brain that need time between sessions, but this hasn't been shown directly for classroom learning.

## Where it comes from

Hermann Ebbinghaus, a German psychologist, first measured forgetting in the 1880s, by learning lists of nonsense syllables and testing himself ([Ebbinghaus, 1885](/papers/ebbinghaus-1885)). He found that spreading his practice over several days needed far fewer repetitions than doing it all at once. Over a century later, in 2006, Nicholas Cepeda and colleagues pulled together hundreds of experiments on the effect ([Cepeda et al., 2006](/papers/cepeda-2006)). Two years later, the same team ran a large study on how long the gaps should be. Spacing works well alongside [retrieval practice](/theories/retrieval-practice): coming back after a gap and trying to remember.
