**The idea in one sentence:** before you teach, check whether people *can't* do the thing, or *can but don't*. Training only fixes the first. The second usually needs a change at work instead.

## You've seen this already

Think of someone who doesn't recycle at home. A talk on how to recycle won't help if they already know how. Perhaps the recycling bin is outside in the rain, collection day keeps changing, or nobody else in the house bothers. Move the bin inside and put the dates on the fridge, and the problem may go away without anyone being taught anything.

Robert Mager and Peter Pipe put it as one question: *could they do it if their life depended on it?* If the answer is yes, it isn't a skills problem, and teaching won't fix it.

## Why it matters for your teaching

In healthcare, the answer to a problem is often "a study day on it". Sometimes that's right. Often it isn't. Say fluid balance charts on a ward aren't being totalled at 24 hours. Before booking a session, ask why:

| If the reason is… | Then… |
|---|---|
| **They don't know how** (a real skill gap) | Teach it, with practice |
| **They knew once, but rarely do it** | A checklist, a pocket card, or a quick practice before they need it |
| **No time, no kit, or the chart is hard to use** | Fix the system, or tell whoever owns it. Teaching won't help |
| **Nobody notices whether it's done** | Feedback: someone checks and says so |
| **They don't see why it matters** | Explain why, briefly. Show what happens to the patient when it's missed |

The same idea runs through other models. Thomas Gilbert argued that most problems at work come from the setting (information, resources and incentives) rather than from what people know (Gilbert, 1978). Cathy Moore's *action mapping* starts training design with a goal the organisation already measures, then asks what people need to do and why they aren't doing it. In medical education, Kern's six steps start the same way: identify the problem and the need before writing a single outcome (Thomas and colleagues, 2022).

## Try this

- **Ask "why aren't they doing it now?"** before anyone mentions a study day. Write down the answer.
- **Ask Mager and Pipe's question.** Could they do it if their life depended on it? If yes, look at the workplace first.
- **Count the cost.** People × hours, plus travel and backfill. What else could those hours buy?
- **Say "not a teaching problem" when it isn't.** And say who should hear about it instead. The [Teaching Design Check](/facilitator/design-check) has a box for exactly that.
- **When it is a skills gap, teach it properly.** Practice, not slides; then follow it up ([what happens back at work](/theories/back-at-work)).

## How sure are we?

This is a way of thinking, not a tested theory. Mager and Pipe's flowchart, Gilbert's model and action mapping all come from practitioners, built from experience rather than controlled studies. There's little research comparing what happens when organisations use them and when they don't.

What they rest on is better supported. Reviews of training at work agree that a needs analysis should come first: what's needed, for whom, and in what setting (Salas and colleagues, 2012).

A common misreading is "never train". That's not the point. When people really can't do something, teaching is the answer, and it deserves to be done well. Many problems are a mix: a skills gap *and* a chart nobody can read. Fix both.

## Where it comes from

Robert Mager and Peter Pipe, American training specialists, published *Analyzing Performance Problems, or You Really Oughta Wanna* in 1970. Its flowchart asks what the gap is, whether it matters, and whether it's a skill problem, before anyone designs training. The third edition came out in 1997. Thomas Gilbert, a psychologist, set out his model of what drives performance at work in *Human Competence* (1978). Cathy Moore, an instructional designer, developed action mapping for workplace training and writes about it on her blog.
