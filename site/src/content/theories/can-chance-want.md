**The idea in one sentence:** people do something when they *can* do it, *get the chance* to, and *want to* at the moment it matters. Training mostly fixes the first. If either of the other two is missing, the behaviour won't happen, however good the teaching.

## You've seen this already

Think of something you mean to do more of: swimming, say. You can swim (you *can*). But the pool's lane sessions clash with your shifts, and nobody you know goes (no *chance*). And after a long day, the sofa wins (you don't *want to*, at least not then). A swimming lesson would fix none of that. A pool open after late shifts and a friend who goes on Tuesdays might.

Susan Michie and her colleagues put this as a simple model, **COM-B**: **C**apability, **O**pportunity and **M**otivation together produce **B**ehaviour (Michie and colleagues, 2011). Take any one away and the behaviour stops.

## Why it matters for your teaching

Each of the three has two sides:

| | What it means | On a ward, it sounds like… | What usually helps |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Capability**: physical | The skill to do it | "I've never actually done one" | Training, with practice |
| **Capability**: psychological | The knowledge, and remembering at the right time | "I didn't know we had to" | Teaching; prompts and checklists |
| **Opportunity**: physical | Time, kit, space, a form that works | "There's never a free monitor" | Change the workplace: kit, rota, form |
| **Opportunity**: social | What people around you do and expect | "Nobody else does it on nights" | Seniors who do it and say so; a team that agrees to |
| **Motivation**: reflective | Believing it matters, planning to do it | "It doesn't change anything for the patient" | Explain why; show the result; set a plan |
| **Motivation**: automatic | Habit and feeling in the moment | "It's 3am and I'm on autopilot" | Make it the easy default; a prompt where it happens; build the habit |

A teaching session reaches the capability rows, and some of reflective motivation. It barely touches opportunity or automatic motivation. That is why a good study day can change what people know, and nothing about what they do.

**It works for organisations too.** Every change needs someone to do something differently, and each of those people has their own C, O and M. Take a manager who agrees to ask staff about the new skill at 30 days. Can they (do they know what to ask)? Do they get the chance (is there a slot for it)? Do they want to (is it something they're judged on)? If you want the workplace to support your learners, run the same three questions for the people who run the workplace ([what happens back at work](/theories/back-at-work)).

Michie's team built a fuller tool around COM-B, the **Behaviour Change Wheel**. Around the centre it lists nine kinds of fix. Two are *education* and *training*. The other seven are persuasion, incentives, coercion, restriction, changing the environment, modelling (someone showing how) and enablement (removing barriers, adding support). Teaching is two options out of nine.

## Try this

- **Name the behaviour exactly.** Who needs to do what, when and where? "Better escalation" is too vague. "The nurse in charge calls the outreach team when a score reaches 5" isn't.
- **Ask the three questions.** Can they? Do they get the chance? Do they want to, at 3am, not just in principle? Ask the people who do the job, not only the person asking for training.
- **Teach only the capability gap.** If the gap is opportunity or motivation, say so, and say who can fix it ([Is training the answer?](/theories/is-training-the-answer)).
- **Fix one opportunity alongside the teaching.** A form, a prompt on the screen, the kit in the right place. It's often cheaper than the session.
- **Run it for the manager too.** What will they need to be able to do, have the chance to do, and want to do, so the learning gets used?
- **Use their words.** Many NHS improvement and public health teams already use COM-B. Saying "training builds capability; the opportunity and motivation sit with the ward" will land with them ([Getting Traction](/facilitator/getting-traction)).

## How sure are we?

COM-B is a way of sorting the causes of behaviour, not a theory that predicts it. It came from reviewing 19 earlier frameworks for behaviour change and drawing out what they shared (Michie and colleagues, 2011). That makes it a good checklist: it helps you notice the cause you would otherwise miss. It doesn't tell you how much each part matters, or what will happen if you change one.

There is little direct evidence that interventions designed with COM-B work better than those designed some other way. Critics have also warned that frameworks like this can turn into box-ticking, and squeeze out judgement and the details of a particular place (Ogden, 2016). Its defenders reply that a shared framework is meant to support judgement, not replace it.

A common misreading is to treat the three letters as labels to stick on a problem, then stop. The point is what comes next: picking the fix that matches the gap. Another is to read COM-B as being about individuals only. The opportunity side is mostly about the workplace, and the people who run it.

## Where it comes from

Susan Michie, Maartje van Stralen and Robert West, health psychologists at University College London, published COM-B and the Behaviour Change Wheel in 2011 in *Implementation Science*. The paper is free to read. Their 2014 book is a step-by-step guide to using it. A related tool, the *Theoretical Domains Framework*, breaks the three into 14 more detailed areas and is widely used in implementation research (Cane and colleagues, 2012). In England, Public Health England based its guides to behaviour change for national and local government on the Behaviour Change Wheel (2020).
