**The idea in one sentence:** decide what people should be able to *do* afterwards, give them practice doing that, and check for that. When those three line up, people learn what you meant them to. When they don't, people learn whatever the check rewards.

## You've seen this already

Think about learning to drive. The goal is to drive safely on real roads. So you practise on real roads, and the test is a drive on real roads with an examiner beside you. The goal, the practice and the check all point at the same thing.

Now imagine the driving test was only a written quiz. Learners would sensibly spend their time memorising the Highway Code, and plenty would pass without ever managing a hill start. Nobody would be lazy or cheating. They would be preparing for the test they were actually given.

## Why it matters for your teaching

The same thing happens in healthcare training all the time. Take a session on a new infusion pump:

| | Out of line | Lined up |
|---|---|---|
| **Outcome:** what should people be able to do? | "Be aware of the new pump" | "Set up the new pump for a patient, safely, without help" |
| **Activity:** what do people do in the session? | Watch twenty slides about it | Set one up in pairs, on the real pump |
| **Check:** how will you know it worked? | Sign the attendance sheet | Watch each person set it up once |

In the left-hand column, everyone ticks the box and nobody has touched the pump. In the right-hand column, the session and the check both rehearse the one thing that matters on the ward.

This is the idea at the heart of Teaching That Sticks: start with what people need to do differently, then build the activity and the check from there.

## Try this

- **Write the outcome as something you could watch.** You can't see someone "understand" or "be aware of" something. You can see them explain, show, choose or set up. Pick the verb first: [Miller's pyramid](/theories/millers-pyramid) helps.
- **Decide how you'll know before you plan the activity.** What would you need to see to be confident it worked? Then plan the practice that gets people there.
- **Ask of every part: does this help people do the outcome?** Slides and talk can set people up, but if nobody ever does the thing, the session isn't finished.
- **Apply it to single slides too.** Ask who each slide is for. Does it help people do the outcome, or is it there to remind you what to say?

## How sure are we?

Constructive alignment is a way of planning, not a single tested finding. It is one of the most widely used ideas in university teaching, and it fits the evidence elsewhere in this library: people get better at what they practise. Direct tests of it are few. One study of university courses found students on better-lined-up courses were more likely to study for understanding rather than just to pass (Wang et al., 2013), but that study relied on surveys, not on what students could do afterwards. Nobody has shown in a trial that it changes what staff do at work. Treat it as a dependable thinking tool, not a guarantee.

You won't always control all three parts. If an exam, a competency sign-off or a mandatory-training system sets the check, you can still line up your outcome and your activity with what people really need to do, and say so if the official check doesn't match.

## Where it comes from

John Biggs, an educational psychologist, gave the idea its name in 1996. He had noticed that students learn what they think they'll be tested on, whatever the teacher intends. He joined two older ideas: that people build their own understanding through what they do (the "constructive" part), and that goals and tests should match (the "alignment" part). His example was a course for working schoolteachers in Hong Kong, who showed what they'd learned by collecting evidence that their own teaching had improved.
