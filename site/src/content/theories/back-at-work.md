**The idea in one sentence:** whether people use what they learned depends on more than the session. It also depends on the person, and on what happens back at work: a chance to try it soon, someone who notices, and nothing pulling them back to the old way.

## You've seen this already

You go on a course and learn a quicker way to do something you do every week. You come back keen. On Monday the shared file still uses the old layout, your manager hasn't heard of the new way, and there's a deadline. You do it the old way, just this once. By Friday the course is a nice memory.

Nothing was wrong with the course. You learned the thing. The week back at work decided what happened next.

## Why it matters for your teaching

In healthcare, the week back at work is often the hardest part. Picture a study day on a new way to escalate a patient who is getting worse. People learn it well. Then they go back to a ward where the observation chart hasn't changed, the senior on shift does it the old way, and nobody asks how it's going.

The research on training at work points to three things that decide whether learning gets used (Baldwin and Ford, 1988):

| | What it means | What helps |
|---|---|---|
| **The person** | Do they believe they can do it, and do they see why it matters? | Explain why it matters to them. Build confidence with practice, not just information |
| **The training** | Did they practise the real thing, in a realistic way? | Practice that looks like the job, with room for mistakes ([Miller's pyramid](/theories/knowing-isnt-doing)) |
| **The workplace** | Will they get the chance, the time and the support to use it? | A chance to use it soon, a manager or colleague who asks, and the form, kit and rota to match |

As the teacher, you control the middle row, and some of the first. The workplace row is why this programme ends with a named person and the support you've agreed with them, not with a feedback form.

## Try this

- **Brief the manager before, not after.** Tell them what's being taught and what you'll ask them to support. A manager who hears about it afterwards, if at all, can't help.
- **Book the first time people will use it.** "On my next shift, at handover, I'll…" ([if-then plans](/theories/if-then-plans)). Plan a chance to use it soon.
- **Fix one thing in the workplace.** If the form, the chart or the kit still fits the old way, change it, or tell whoever owns it.
- **Ask what got in the way.** At about 30 days, ask what people changed, what happened, and what got in the way. The answers to the last question are a to-do list for the workplace, not a mark against the learner ([Kirkpatrick](/theories/did-the-training-work)).
- **Come back to it.** A reminder, a quiz at the huddle, a team message: [spacing it out](/theories/spread-it-out) helps it last.

## How sure are we?

Fairly sure about the main point; less sure about the details. Reviews that pool many studies agree that the person, the training and the workplace all matter (Blume and colleagues, 2010; Grossman and Salas, 2011). Support from managers and colleagues, and the chance to use what was learned, come up again and again.

But much of this research asks people afterwards whether they used their training, so it leans on self-report. Most of it comes from workplaces outside healthcare. And it is better at saying what matters than at saying how much each thing matters.

A common misreading is that "only 10% of training is ever used at work". That figure was never measured: it can be traced to a passing remark in a 1982 magazine article, repeated until it sounded like a finding (Ford and colleagues, 2011). How much transfers depends on the training, the person and the workplace.

## Where it comes from

Timothy Baldwin and Kevin Ford, American researchers in workplace learning, reviewed the research in 1988 and set out the three inputs: the person, the training and the work environment. Their model is still the usual starting point. Later reviews pooled the studies that followed (Blume and colleagues, 2010, with 89 studies) and summarised what matters most for people who design training (Grossman and Salas, 2011; Salas and colleagues, 2012). The New World Kirkpatrick Model puts the same idea into practice with "required drivers": the processes at work that reinforce, check, encourage and reward the new behaviour.
