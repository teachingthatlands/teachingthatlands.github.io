**The idea in one sentence:** "active learning" isn't one thing you either do or don't do. It's a scale, and the further people go with an idea, from listening, to doing something with it, to explaining it in their own words, to working it through with someone else, the more they tend to learn.

## You've seen this already

Think about learning a new recipe from a cookery show.

- You **watch** it. You nod along. A week later you remember it had garlic in.
- You **write the steps down** as you watch. A bit more sticks.
- You **explain it to a friend in your own words**, including why you fry the onions first. Now you've had to make sense of it.
- You **cook it together**, and your friend asks, "Why not add the garlic now?" You have to think it through, and you both end up understanding it better.

Each step asks more of your head than the one before. That's the scale.

## Why it matters for your teaching

It's easy to think a session is active because people are busy. But copying a slide into a notebook is busy, not deep. What counts is what's going on in their heads.

Take a ten-minute slot on a new sepsis screening tool at a team huddle:

| | What people do | Where it sits |
|---|---|---|
| **Passive** | Listen while you talk through the tool | Takes it in |
| **Active** | Tick through the tool on a printed example | Handles it |
| **Constructive** | Explain to a partner *why* this patient scores as they do | Makes sense of it |
| **Interactive** | In pairs, disagree about a borderline case and agree what to do | Builds on each other's thinking |

You don't always need the top of the scale. A 6am handover may only have room for one quick "explain it back to me". But that one question moves people from listening to making sense of it.

This is the idea at the heart of Teaching That Sticks: start with what people need to do differently, then pick the step on the scale that gets them practising it.

## Try this

- **Ask "what will they be doing in minute three?"** If the honest answer is "listening", find one place to move them up a step.
- **Swap "any questions?" for "explain it back".** "Tell me in your own words why we check this first" makes people make sense of it.
- **Make pair work a real exchange.** Give pairs something to disagree about or decide together. Two people reading the same handout side by side is still only active.
- **Use the setting you have.** On a busy shift, one "why?" question can do more than a long activity you never get to run.

## How sure are we?

Fairly sure about the broad pattern; less sure about the fine detail. Many studies show that people learn more when they explain, question and build on ideas than when they only listen, and a large review of university science courses found active approaches beat lecturing alone ([Freeman et al., 2014](/papers/active-beats-lecturing)). One classroom study found an extra gain from the interactive step over the constructive one, but it was small.

The tidy four-step order is less certain. Critics point out that you can't always tell from the outside which step someone is on: a person sitting quietly may be thinking hard, and a busy group may be chatting about something else. Discussion only helps if people really build on each other's ideas. Most of the evidence comes from schools and universities, not from healthcare workplaces.

One myth to avoid: the "learning pyramid" that says we remember 5% of lectures, 50% of discussion and 90% of what we teach. Those numbers have never been found in any study (Letrud, 2012). The scale here makes no promise about percentages.

## Where it comes from

Michelene Chi, an American learning scientist, set out the idea in 2009 and, with Ruth Wylie, in a widely cited 2014 paper. They called it ICAP, after its four steps from top to bottom: Interactive, Constructive, Active, Passive. They built it by looking back across many earlier studies of things like note-taking, concept mapping and explaining to yourself, and sorting them by what learners were doing in their heads.
